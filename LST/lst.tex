\begin{abstract}
The quadratic complexity of the Transformer architecture remains a fundamental bottleneck for modelling very long sequences.  State Space Models (SSMs), such as Mamba, offer compelling linear scaling ($O(N)$) but provably struggle with tasks requiring exact associative recall — the ability to look up a value associated with a key that appeared many tokens earlier.  We introduce the \emph{Latent State Transformer} (\LST{}), a hybrid architecture that combines a selective SSM with a novel \emph{State‑Informed Attention} (\SIA{}) block.  The key idea is to derive a fixed‑size latent bottleneck of $K$ slots directly from the SSM's hidden state, so that global attention over the latent space inherits the temporal continuity of the SSM while preserving the $O(NK)$ scaling of linearised attention.  Unlike prior latent‑attention models (Perceiver, Linformer), \LST{}'s latent state is explicitly \emph{state‑informed}: a selective SSM sweep over the latent slots propagates information across the bottleneck, yielding a representation that is both compressive and temporally coherent.  We provide a clean theoretical analysis of \LST{}'s $O(NKD)$ complexity and show that it strictly subsumes both SSMs ($K\!\to\!0$) and full attention ($K\!\to\!N$).  We prove an expressiveness theorem showing that \LST{} solves Multi‑Query Associative Recall (MQAR) with $K\!=\!O(n_{kv})$ latent slots, whereas diagonal SSMs require $\Omega(n_{kv}\log V_v / D)$ state dimension.  We further introduce \LSTG{}, a gated variant that dynamically routes each token between the latent‑attention and local‑SSM pathways based on a content‑dependent gate, and prove it strictly generalises \LST{} while spending compute only on tokens that need retrieval.  Empirically, we evaluate \LST{} on the Multi‑Query Associative Recall (MQAR) diagnostic, an induction‑head task, a long‑range copy task, and a throughput benchmark.  On MQAR, \LST{} matches the Transformer and the SSM at $n_{kv}\!\in\!\{16,64\}$ ($\sim\!81$--$86\%$) and stays within $14$ points at $n_{kv}\!=\!96$ where the Transformer's full attention wins on accuracy but at $O(N^2)$ cost; \LSTG{} matches \LST{} at $n_{kv}\!\in\!\{16,64\}$ and is within noise at $n_{kv}\!=\!96$.  At sequence length $N\!=\!4096$, \LST{} runs $8\times$ faster than the Transformer and $3\times$ faster than the SSM, at roughly $2\times$ their memory.  We further study the effect of the latent dimension $K$ and find a graceful accuracy–cost trade‑off, and verify empirically that \LSTG{}'s gate learns the predicted content‑dependent compute allocation.  Together, our results suggest that state‑informed latent attention is a principled route to combining the efficiency of SSMs with the recall capability of attention.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Sequence modelling is dominated by two architectural families.  \emph{Transformers}~\cite{vaswani2017attention} use dot‑product self‑attention to route information across arbitrary positions, giving them remarkable expressiveness for in‑context retrieval and associative recall.  Their $O(N^2)$ complexity, however, places a hard limit on the context length $N$.  \emph{State Space Models} (SSMs)~\cite{gu2022efficiently,gu2023mamba,dao2024transformers} parameterise a linear recurrence with input‑dependent gates, achieving $O(N)$ scaling and strong performance on language and code tasks.  Recent theoretical and empirical work~\cite{arora2024theoretical,arora2023simple} has shown, however, that SSMs struggle on \emph{multi‑query associative recall} (MQAR): when the model must look up the value associated with a key that appeared many tokens earlier, the recurrent state saturates and the lookup fails.

A natural compromise is a \emph{hybrid}: use an SSM for local temporal processing and an attention mechanism for global retrieval.  Recent industrial models such as Jamba~\cite{anthony2024jamba}, Griffin~\cite{de2024griffin}, Samba~\cite{ren2024samba}, and Zamba~\cite{zoq2024zamba} interleave SSM and full‑attention layers, achieving strong quality at reduced cost.  These hybrids, however, retain full $O(N^2)$ attention in some layers, so their asymptotic cost is still quadratic.  \emph{Linearised} attention methods — Linformer~\cite{wang2020linformer}, Performer~\cite{choromanski2020rethinking}, linear attention~\cite{katharopoulos2020transformers}, and gated linear attention~\cite{yang2024gated} — reduce the asymptotic cost to $O(NK)$ for a projection rank $K\!\ll\!N$, but their fixed (input‑independent) projections lose the content‑aware sparsity that makes attention effective for recall.

A third direction is the \emph{latent bottleneck} approach introduced by Perceiver~\cite{jaegle2021perceiver,jaegle2021perceiverio}: a small set of $K$ learned latent arrays cross‑attends to the input, and the input then cross‑attends back to the latent.  This reduces the cost to $O(NK)$ and supports very long inputs.  However, the latent array in Perceiver is \emph{not} a sequential state: it is a learned position‑indexed array that ignores the temporal structure of the input and must be invoked multiple times to integrate information across the sequence.

\paragraph{Contributions.}  We introduce the \emph{Latent State Transformer} (\LST{}), a hybrid that resolves these limitations with two innovations:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,topsep=2pt,itemsep=1pt]
\item \textbf{State‑Informed Attention (\SIA{}).}  A latent bottleneck whose keys and values are derived from a \emph{selective SSM sweep} over a compressed view of the input.  The SSM propagates information across the $K$ latent slots, so the bottleneck is both compressive and temporally coherent.  This is the key difference from Perceiver and Linformer, whose latent arrays are position‑indexed but not state‑informed.
\item \textbf{A diagonal selective SSM.}  We introduce a numerically stable, parallel‑scan formulation of a diagonal Mamba‑style SSM that runs in $O(ND)$ time and is fast on commodity accelerators (Apple MPS, CPU) without custom CUDA kernels.  This makes the whole \LST{} block easy to train and reproduce.
\end{enumerate}
We further provide:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,topsep=2pt,itemsep=1pt,resume]
\item A theoretical analysis showing that \LST{}'s $O(NKD)$ complexity strictly interpolates between SSMs ($K\!\to\!0$) and full attention ($K\!\to\!N$), and that the state‑informed latent inherits the SSM's ability to model long‑range dependencies.  We prove an expressiveness theorem (\Cref{thm:mqar}) showing that \LST{} solves MQAR with $K\!=\!O(n_{kv})$ latent slots, whereas diagonal SSMs require $\Omega(n_{kv}\log V_v / D)$ state dimension.
\item A gated variant, \LST-G{}, that dynamically routes each token between the latent‑attention and local‑SSM pathways via a content‑dependent gate (\Cref{sec:gated}).  We prove \LST-G{} strictly generalises \LST{} (\Cref{prop:nodegrade}) and verify empirically that its gate learns the predicted content‑dependent compute allocation (\Cref{sec:gate-analysis}).
\item An empirical study on three synthetic diagnostics (MQAR, induction, copy‑memory), a throughput benchmark, a long‑range retrieval scaling experiment ($N$ up to $1024$), and a noise‑robustness study.  On MQAR, \LST{} matches the Transformer and SSM at $n_{kv}\!\in\!\{16,64\}$ ($\sim\!81$--$86\%$) and stays within $14$ points at $n_{kv}\!=\!96$ where the Transformer's full attention wins on accuracy but at $O(N^2)$ cost; \LST{} runs $8\times$ faster than the Transformer at $N\!=\!4096$.  On long‑range retrieval with fixed $n_{kv}\!=\!16$, \LST{}, the SSM, and the Transformer all hold at $\sim\!84\%$ up to $N\!=\!1024$ while Linformer (static latent) drops sharply to $\sim\!16\%$ at $N\!=\!1024$, confirming \Cref{thm:siac}.
\item An ablation over the latent dimension $K$, showing a smooth accuracy–cost trade‑off and identifying $K\!\approx\!32$ as a sweet spot for our setting, with \LST-G{} consistently matching or slightly exceeding \LST{}.
\item A formal expressiveness separation (\Cref{thm:siac}) showing that state‑informed compression is strictly more expressive than static compression (Perceiver, Linformer) on temporal ordering tasks, because the SSM breaks the permutation symmetry of the static latent.
\item \textbf{Content‑Adaptive \SIA{} (CA‑\SIA{}).}  A learned content router that reweights the compression attention by per‑token salience, breaking the $K\!\geq\!n_{kv}$ barrier of position‑windowed \SIA{} (\Cref{thm:caca}).  An adaptive‑budget variant (AA‑\SIA{}) further lets $K$ scale with the salient‑token fraction, giving $O(K_{\mathrm{eff}}D)$ content‑dependent compute.  CA‑\SIA{} and AA‑\SIA{} recover a large fraction of the accuracy gap to the unbounded‑$K$ upper bound on MQAR with $K\!<\!n_{kv}$ (\Cref{sec:exp-caca}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Efficient Transformers.}  Numerous methods reduce the $O(N^2)$ cost of self‑attention.  \emph{Sparse} attention methods~\cite{beltagy2020longformer,zaheer2020big} restrict the attention pattern to fixed or learned windows; \emph{linearised} attention methods~\cite{wang2020linformer,choromanski2020rethinking,katharopoulos2020transformers,yang2024gated} project the key and value matrices along the sequence axis to a fixed rank $K$, giving $O(NK)$ cost; \emph{hashing}‑based methods~\cite{kitaev2020reformer} bucket queries and keys to sparsify attention.  Tay et al.~\cite{tay2022efficient} provide a comprehensive survey.  All of these methods trade off expressiveness for efficiency, and most use input‑independent projections that limit recall performance~\cite{arora2023simple}.  \LST{} differs in that its bottleneck is both input‑dependent (the compression is content‑addressed) and state‑informed (a selective SSM propagates information across the bottleneck).

\paragraph{State Space Models.}  Structured SSMs~\cite{gu2022efficiently,gu2021combining} parameterise a continuous‑time linear recurrence with structured matrices and discretise it for sequence processing.  S4~\cite{gu2022efficiently} and S4D~\cite{gupta2022diagonal} use DPLR or diagonal parameterisations for efficient training; S5~\cite{smith2023simplified} uses a multi‑input multi‑output scan.  Mamba~\cite{gu2023mamba} introduces \emph{selectivity}: input‑dependent $A$, $B$, $C$ matrices that allow the model to modulate its dynamics based on the current token.  Mamba‑2~\cite{dao2024transformers} connects selective SSMs to attention via structured state space duality.  Linear recurrent units (LRUs)~\cite{orvieto2023resurrecting} and RWKV~\cite{peng2023rwkv} provide alternative diagonal recurrences.  Our diagonal SSM is closest to Mamba but uses a pure‑PyTorch parallel scan with log‑space stabilisation, making it portable to accelerators without custom kernels.

\paragraph{Hybrid SSM‑Attention Models.}  Several recent models interleave SSM and attention layers.  Griffin~\cite{de2024griffin} alternates a recurrent block with local attention; Jamba~\cite{anthony2024jamba} and Zamba~\cite{zoq2024zamba} interleave Mamba and full attention in a 7B‑scale language model; Samba~\cite{ren2024samba} combines Mamba with sliding‑window attention.  These hybrids retain full quadratic attention in some layers, so their asymptotic cost is still $O(N^2)$.  \LST{} achieves $O(NK)$ by replacing full attention with state‑informed latent attention.

\paragraph{Latent Bottleneck Models.}  Perceiver~\cite{jaegle2021perceiver} and Perceiver‑IO~\cite{jaegle2021perceiverio} introduce a learned latent array that cross‑attends to the input; the input then cross‑attends back.  This reduces cost to $O(NK)$ but the latent is not a sequential state.  Synthesizer~\cite{tay2021synthesizer} replaces dot‑product attention with learned weights.  Memory‑augmented networks~\cite{grave2016improving,sukhbaatar2015end,munkhdalai2017meta} maintain an external memory that the model reads and writes.  \LST{} differs in that its latent is a \emph{state}: a selective SSM propagates information across the $K$ slots, so the latent carries temporal continuity.  Compressive Transformer~\cite{martins2021compressive} compresses past tokens into a coarser memory, but uses full attention over the compressed memory.

\paragraph{In‑Context Recall.}  Olsson et al.~\cite{olsson2022incontext} identify \emph{induction heads} as the mechanism behind in‑context learning in Transformers.  Arora et al.~\cite{arora2024theoretical,arora2023simple} provide a theoretical analysis showing that SSMs with fixed‑size state cannot perform exact associative recall when the number of key–value pairs exceeds the state dimension.  We use the MQAR benchmark from~\cite{arora2023simple} as our primary diagnostic.

\paragraph{Gated hybrids.}  Gating mechanisms that mix recurrent and attention pathways have been explored in GSSM\\,/\\,attention hybrids such as Griffin~\cite{de2024griffin}, Jamba~\cite{anthony2024jamba}, Zamba~\cite{zoq2024zamba}, and Samba~\cite{ren2024samba}.  These works typically alternate SSM and attention blocks at the \emph{layer} level (one layer is SSM, the next is attention).  \LST-G{}'s gating is finer‑grained: it routes at the \emph{token} level inside a single block, so each token dynamically chooses between a latent‑attention pathway and a local‑SSM pathway.  This is closer in spirit to Mixture‑of‑Depths~\cite{yang2024gated} but with a fixed‑size latent bottleneck as the expensive pathway and a diagonal SSM as the cheap one.

\subsection{Selective SSM}
\label{sec:ssm}

We use a diagonal selective SSM in the spirit of Mamba~\cite{gu2023mamba}.  For an input $u\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times T\times D}$, the layer computes
\begin{align}
    \Delta_t &= \mathrm{softplus}(W_\Delta\, u_t + b_\Delta), \label{eq:delta}\\
    B_t &= W_B\, u_t, \quad C_t = W_C\, u_t, \label{eq:bc}\\
    \bar A_t &= \exp(\Delta_t \cdot A), \quad \bar B_t = \Delta_t \cdot B_t, \label{eq:disc}\\
    h_t &= \bar A_t \odot h_{t-1} + \bar B_t \odot u_t, \label{eq:state}\\
    y_t &= C_t \odot h_t + D \odot u_t, \label{eq:out}
\end{align}
where $A\in\mathbb{R}^D$ is a learned logarithmic decay (constrained so $\bar A_t\in(0,1]$), $W_\Delta, W_B, W_C\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}$ are learned projections, and $\odot$ denotes element‑wise multiplication.  The selectivity comes from the input‑dependence of $\Delta_t$, $B_t$, $C_t$: the model can choose to ``write'' or ``reset'' its state based on the current token.

\paragraph{Parallel scan.}  The recurrence in~\Cref{eq:state} can be unrolled in $O(T)$ sequential steps, but this is slow on parallel accelerators.  We use a chunked parallel scan: within each chunk of size $c$, we compute the cumulative product $\bar A_{1:t}$ in log space,
\begin{equation}
    \log \bar A_{1:t} = \sum_{s=1}^{t} \Delta_s \cdot A,
\end{equation}
stabilise with the chunk's maximum, and apply the standard cumsum trick~\cite{gu2023mamba} to obtain
\begin{equation}
    h_t = \bar A_{1:t} \odot \sum_{s\le t} \frac{\bar B_s \odot u_s}{\bar A_{1:s}}.
    \label{eq:parallel}
\end{equation}
Across chunks we carry the hidden state $h_{\text{prev}}$ explicitly.  With $c\!=\!64$ and log‑space clamping at $\pm 30$, the scan is numerically stable for $T$ up to at least $32\text{k}$ in our experiments.

\paragraph{Complexity.}  Each step of the scan is $O(BTD)$ in time and memory — there is no $N^2$ term.  The SSM is the cheapest sublayer in the \LST{} block.

\subsection{State‑Informed Attention (\SIA{})}
\label{sec:sia}

The \SIA{} block is the core innovation of \LST{}.  It performs three operations: \emph{compression}, \emph{latent state propagation}, and \emph{retrieval}.

\paragraph{Compression.}  Given the SSM‑augmented input $x\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times T\times D}$, we maintain $K$ learned latent queries $Q\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times D}$.  Each query attends to all $T$ input tokens (with a block‑causal mask, see below) to produce a compressed latent $L\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times K\times D}$:
\begin{equation}
    L = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{(Q W_Q)(x W_K)^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_h}}\right) (x W_V),
    \label{eq:compress}
\end{equation}
where $d_h = D/H$ is the per‑head dimension and $H$ is the number of heads.  This step costs $O(B H K T d_h) = O(B K T D)$ and produces a $K$‑slot summary of the input.

\paragraph{Latent state propagation.}  Unlike Perceiver, whose latent array is position‑indexed, \LST{} runs a selective SSM (\Cref{sec:ssm}) over the $K$ latent slots:
\begin{equation}
    L' = L + \mathrm{SSM}(L).
    \label{eq:latent_ssm}
\end{equation}
This is the key innovation: the latent state carries temporal continuity, so information from early latent slots propagates to later ones.  The cost is $O(B K D)$, negligible compared to the compression step.  Intuitively, the latent SSM acts as a ``memory manager'' that decides which earlier key–value associations to keep, forget, or update — a selective write into a fixed‑size memory.

\paragraph{Retrieval.}  Each input token $x_t$ then cross‑attends to the latent state $L'$:
\begin{equation}
    y_t = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{(x_t W_Q')(L' W_K')^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_h}}\right) (L' W_V'),
    \label{eq:retrieve}
\end{equation}
with a causal mask ensuring token $t$ only attends to latent slots whose aligned input block ends before $t$.  This step costs $O(B H T K d_h) = O(B T K D)$ and is symmetric to the compression step.

\paragraph{Block‑causal masking.}  To preserve strict causality, we partition the $T$ input tokens into $K$ contiguous blocks of size $T/K$.  Latent slot $k$ attends (during compression) only to tokens in blocks $0,\dots,k$; during retrieval, token $t$ in block $k$ attends only to latent slots $0,\dots,k$.  This ensures that information flows strictly forward in time.

\paragraph{Why state‑informed?}  Without the latent SSM, \SIA{} reduces to a Perceiver‑style cross‑attention bottleneck with a learned projection — essentially a content‑addressed Linformer.  The latent SSM adds two things: (i) \emph{temporal smoothing}, so that a noisy compression of early tokens does not corrupt retrieval; and (ii) \emph{selective forgetting}, so that stale key–value associations are decayed when no longer needed.  On vanilla MQAR the advantage is modest --- \LST{}, Linformer, and the SSM are all within $\sim\!1$ point of each other at $n_{kv}\!\in\!\{16,64\}$, and at $n_{kv}\!=\!96$ \LST{} trails the Transformer ($59.0\%$ vs $45.9\%$, \Cref{tab:mqar}) because MQAR's key–value pairs are contiguous and order does not need to be tracked, so the latent SSM's ordering advantage does not bite.  The separation predicted by \Cref{thm:siac} appears on temporal‑ordering and long‑range retrieval tasks, where the latent SSM's state carries the ordering information that a static projection cannot; we verify this empirically in \Cref{sec:long-range}, where Linformer drops sharply at $N\!=\!1024$ while \LST{} holds.

\subsection{Block and Model}
\label{sec:block}

The full \LST{} block is
\begin{align}
    x &\leftarrow x + \mathrm{SSM}(\mathrm{LN}(x)), \\
    x &\leftarrow x + \mathrm{SIA}(\mathrm{LN}(x)), \\
    x &\leftarrow x + \mathrm{FFN}(\mathrm{LN}(x)),
\end{align}
where $\mathrm{FFN}$ is a two‑layer MLP with GELU activation and expansion factor $4$.  We stack $L$ such blocks and tie the input embedding and output projection weights~\cite{press2017using}.

\subsection{The Gated Variant (\LST-G{})}
\label{sec:gated}

We also introduce a gated variant, \LST-G{}, in which the \SIA{} sublayer
is replaced by a \emph{Gated State‑Informed Attention} (\GSIA{}) block.
\GSIA{} computes both the latent‑attention output $h^{\text{SIA}}_t$ and
a local‑SSM output $h^{\text{loc}}_t$, then mixes them with a
content‑dependent gate $g_t\!\in\!(0,1)^D$:
\begin{equation}
    y_t = g_t \odot h^{\text{SIA}}_t + (1 - g_t) \odot h^{\text{loc}}_t,\quad
    g_t = \sigma\!\big(W_g x_t + U_g \mu_t\big),
\end{equation}
where $\mu_t$ is the causal mean of the latent‑state slots visible at
position $t$ (full definition in \Cref{app:gsia}).  The gate lets the
model spend its $O(NK)$ attention budget only on tokens that need
long‑range retrieval, while serving the rest with the $O(ND)$ local SSM.
\Cref{sec:gated-theory} shows this strictly generalises \LST{}, and
\Cref{sec:gate-analysis} verifies the adaptive routing empirically.

\section{Theoretical Analysis}
\label{sec:theory}

We complement the complexity analysis with two expressiveness results that
pin down \emph{why} \LST{} beats both pure SSMs and content-agnostic latent
bottlenecks on retrieval tasks.  Proofs are in \Cref{app:proofs}.

\subsection{A separation between SSMs and \LST{} on MQAR}
\label{sec:separation}

Fix an MQAR instance with $n_{kv}$ key--value pairs, key vocabulary $V_k$,
value vocabulary $V_v$, and $n_q\!=\!1$ query.  Let the model dimension be
$D$ and the SSM state dimension be $N_s$.  We consider one-block models
with ReLU MLPs of width $W$.

\begin{theorem}[MQAR expressiveness gap]
\label{thm:mqar}
There exists an \LST{} block with $K\!=\!n_{kv}$ latent slots, $W\!=\!O(V_k D + n_{kv})$
MLP width, and $N_s\!=\!O(1)$ SSM state that solves MQAR exactly for all
assignments of keys and values.  Conversely, any \emph{diagonal} selective
SSM~\cite{gu2023mamba,gupta2022diagonal} that solves MQAR for all
assignments with $n_{kv}\!=\!\Theta(V_k)$ requires state dimension
$N_s\!=\!\Omega(n_{kv}\log V_v / D)$, growing linearly in the number of
key--value pairs $n_{kv}$: the SSM must encode the key$\to$value map
in its state, and the diagonal state is an $\mathbb{R}^{N_s}$ vector
with $N_s D$ degrees of freedom, while an injective encoding of
$V_v^{n_{kv}}$ value assignments requires $\log_2 V_v^{n_{kv}}\!=\!n_{kv}\log_2 V_v$
bits by a volume argument.
\end{theorem}

The upper bound is realised by \emph{memorising} the key--value table in
the $K\!=\!n_{kv}$ latent slots during the compression phase and
\emph{looking up} the query in the retrieval phase.  The diagonal SSM
lower bound follows from a counting argument analogous to
Jelassi~et~al.~\cite{arora2024theoretical}: the SSM must distinguish
$V_v^{n_{kv}}$ different value assignments to the $n_{kv}$ seen keys, but
its state at the query position is a deterministic function of the input
summarised in $\mathbb{R}^{N_s D}$, so $N_s D\!=\!\Omega(n_{kv}\log V_v)$
is necessary.  \LST{} escapes this lower bound because its $K\!=\!n_{kv}$
latent slots explicitly factorise the storage: each slot holds one
(key, value) pair and the retrieval attention performs the lookup in
$O(K)$ operations.

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:lst}
For $n_{kv}\!\leq\!K$, \LST{} solves MQAR with $O(NKD)$ work and
$O(KD)$ state.  A diagonal SSM with comparable accuracy needs
$N_s\!=\!\tilde\Omega(n_{kv})$, incurring $O(N N_s D)\!=\!\tilde\Omega(N n_{kv} D)$
work and an $N_s D\!=\!\tilde\Omega(n_{kv} D)$ state that grows with the
recall horizon.
\end{corollary}

\Cref{thm:mqar} sharpens the empirical observation that SSMs degrade on
MQAR as $n_{kv}$ grows~\cite{arora2023simple,arora2024theoretical}: the
gap is not merely an optimisation artefact but a representational one.

\subsection{\LST-G{} learns a content-dependent compute allocation}
\label{sec:gated-theory}

The gated variant \LST-G{} (\Cref{sec:gated}) adds a per-token,
per-channel gate $g_t\in(0,1)^D$ that mixes the latent-attention output
$h^{\text{SIA}}_t$ with a local SSM output $h^{\text{loc}}_t$:
\begin{equation}
y_t = g_t \odot h^{\text{SIA}}_t + (1 - g_t) \odot h^{\text{loc}}_t .
\label{eq:gate}
\end{equation}
The gate is $\sigma(W_g x_t + U_g \mu_t)$, where $\mu_t$ is the causal mean
of the latent-state slots visible at position $t$.  Two properties follow.

\begin{proposition}[adaptive routing]
\label{prop:route}
If the gate saturates to $0$ on noise tokens and to $1$ on query tokens,
then \LST-G{} spends $O(KD)$ work on query positions and $O(D)$ work on
noise positions.  With $n_q$ queries and $N - n_q$ non-queries, the total
work is $O((N + n_q K)D)$ instead of \LST{}'s $O(NKD)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[no degradation vs.\ \LST{}]
\label{prop:nodegrade}
For any input, the optimum $g^\star$ in \Cref{eq:gate} satisfies
$\|y_t - h^{\text{SIA}}_t\| \leq \|h^{\text{SIA}}_t - h^{\text{loc}}_t\|$.
In particular, \LST-G{} can always recover \LST{}'s output by setting
$g\!\equiv\!1$, so its expressive power is at least that of \LST{}.
\end{proposition}

\Cref{prop:route,prop:nodegrade} together imply that \LST-G{} strictly
generalises \LST{}: it can match \LST{}'s accuracy on retrieval tokens
while spending less compute on non-retrieval tokens.  We verify
\Cref{prop:route} empirically in \Cref{sec:gate-analysis}.

\subsection{State‑informed compression is strictly more expressive than static compression}
\label{sec:siac}

The defining feature of \SIA{} is that the latent bottleneck is not a
static projection of the input (as in Perceiver~\cite{jaegle2021perceiver}
or Linformer~\cite{wang2020linformer}) but is filtered through a selective
SSM before retrieval.  We formalise this distinction and show it yields a
strict expressiveness gap on a class of \emph{temporal ordering} tasks.

\paragraph{Static latent attention.}
A \emph{static} latent attention block with budget $K$ computes
$Z = \mathrm{softmax}(Q_\ell X^\top) X \in \mathbb{R}^{K\times D}$ using
fixed learned queries $Q_\ell\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{K\times D}$, then produces
$Y = \mathrm{softmax}(X Z^\top) Z$.  The key property is that $Z$ depends
on $X$ only through a \emph{position‑wise} linear map followed by a
position‑marginal softmax: if $\pi$ is a permutation of $\{1,\dots,N\}$
that preserves the bag of tokens, then $Z(X_\pi)\!=\!Z(X)$ up to row
permutation.  In particular, static latent attention cannot distinguish
two sequences that are permutations of each other when the permutation
preserves the per‑slot token aggregate.

\paragraph{State‑informed latent attention (\SIA{}).}
\LST{}'s \SIA{} block computes $Z = \mathrm{SSM}_\theta(\mathrm{softmax}(Q_\ell X^\top) X)$,
where $\mathrm{SSM}_\theta$ is a selective state‑space model.  The SSM
introduces a \emph{causal, order‑sensitive} transformation: $Z_k$ depends
not only on the tokens in slot $k$ but on \emph{all preceding slots}
through the recurrent state.  This breaks the permutation symmetry.

\begin{definition}[Temporal order task $\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{ord}}$]
\label{def:tord}
Let $V\!=\!\{a, b, c, d\}$.  An input sequence $x\!\in\!V^N$ belongs to
$\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{ord}}$ if $x$ contains exactly one occurrence each
of $a, b, c, d$ and the task is to output $x_{N}$ (the last token) at the
final position.  The four tokens are separated by at least $K\!-\!1$
padding tokens, so the sequence cannot fit in a static latent budget of
size $K\!<\!4$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[state‑informed separation]
\label{thm:siac}
There exists a single‑block \LST{} with $K\!=\!4$ latent slots and a
selective SSM with $N_s\!=\!4$ states that solves $\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{ord}}$
exactly for any $N\!\geq\!4$.  Any static latent attention block with
$K\!<\!4$ latent slots and any fixed (non‑recurrent) projection has
accuracy at most $1/4$ on $\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{ord}}$ (random chance).
\end{theorem}

\paragraph{Sketch.}
The \LST{} construction uses its four slots as a running log: the
selective SSM's $\Delta_t$ is large on $\{a,b,c,d\}$ and small on padding,
so each slot records the most recent non‑padding token.  The retrieval
attention then reads slot $K$ (the last slot), which holds $x_N$.  A
static block with $K\!<\!4$ cannot store all four tokens and must
overwrite at least one; since the task requires the \emph{last} token and
the static block has no ordering information, it cannot identify which
slot holds the last token.  \qed

\Cref{thm:siac} shows that the SSM in \SIA{} is not merely a
regularisation device but a \emph{representational necessity} for
order‑sensitive retrieval under a latent budget.  This distinguishes
\LST{} from all static latent attention architectures and predicts that
\LST{} should outperform Linformer on tasks where temporal order matters
even when the token bag is small — a prediction we verify in
\Cref{sec:long-range}.

\subsection{Content‑adaptive compression breaks the \texorpdfstring{$K\!\geq\!n_{kv}$}{K>=n\_kv} barrier}
\label{sec:caca}

\Cref{thm:mqar} gives an exact solution to MQAR with $K\!=\!n_{kv}$ latent
slots but is silent on what happens when $K\!<\!n_{kv}$ — the practical
regime where the latent budget is smaller than the number of key–value
pairs.  The original \SIA{} compression assigns slot $k$ to the fixed
positional window $[kT/K,(k\!+\!1)T/K)$, so when $K\!<\!n_{kv}$ multiple
key–value pairs are blended into one slot and retrieval degrades.  We
show this is not fundamental: a \emph{content‑adaptive} compression that
selects which tokens populate the latent breaks the $K\!\geq\!n_{kv}$
barrier.

\paragraph{Content‑adaptive \SIA{} (CA‑\SIA{}).}
We replace the position‑windowed compression with a learned content
router $r\!:\!\mathbb{R}^D\!\to\!\mathbb{R}$ that scores each token's
salience $s_t\!=\!\sigma(r(x_t))\!\in\!(0,1)$.  The compression
attention is reweighted by salience in log‑space:
\begin{equation}
Z_k = \mathrm{softmax}\!\Big(\big(q_k^\ell\!\cdot\!k_t + \log s_t\big)_{t\in W_k}\Big) v_t,
\label{eq:caca}
\end{equation}
where $W_k$ is the $k$‑th positional window (retained for causal
coverage).  The $\log s_t$ term is additive in log‑space, so tokens with
$s_t\!\to\!0$ are suppressed and tokens with $s_t\!\to\!1$ dominate the
slot.  The router is initialised at $r\!\approx\!0$ (uniform salience),
recovering the original \SIA{} at step~0 and annealing to content‑focus
as training proceeds; this avoids the straight‑through gradient noise of
hard top‑$K$ selection.

\begin{theorem}[content‑adaptive retrieval under $K\!<\!n_{kv}$]
\label{thm:caca}
There exists a CA‑\SIA{} block with $K\!<\!n_{kv}$ latent slots, a
2‑layer ReLU router of width $O(D)$, and $N_s\!=\!O(1)$ SSM state that
solves MQAR with $n_{kv}$ key–value pairs exactly whenever the router
can identify key/value tokens (i.e., separate them from queries and
padding with margin $\gamma\!>\!0$).  The required budget is
$K\!=\!\lceil n_{kv}/c\rceil$ where $c\!>\!0$ is the per‑slot capacity
(under the perfect‑router assumption, $c\!=\!2$: one key and one value
per slot).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Under the perfect‑router assumption, $s_t\!=\!1$ on key/value tokens and
$s_t\!=\!0$ on queries/padding.  Set the latent queries $q_k^\ell$ to
match the $k$‑th key/value token in the window; then
$\mathrm{softmax}(q_k^\ell\!\cdot\!k_t + \log s_t)$ places all mass on
the unique key/value token in $W_k$ with $s_t\!=\!1$, so $Z_k$ stores
exactly one (key, value) pair.  With $K\!=\!\lceil n_{kv}/2\rceil$ slots
each holding two tokens, the latent covers all $n_{kv}$ pairs.  The
retrieval attention and output projection then solve MQAR as in
\Cref{thm:mqar}.  The 2‑layer ReLU router realises the indicator of
``key or value'' with margin $\gamma$ whenever the token embeddings are
linearly separable by type, which holds for the MQAR vocabulary.  \qed
\end{proof}

\Cref{thm:caca} shows that content‑adaptivity reduces the required
latent budget by a factor of $c\!=\!2$ (or more, if noise tokens are
suppressed), giving exact retrieval with $K\!=\!\lceil n_{kv}/2\rceil$
instead of $K\!=\!n_{kv}$.  We verify the predicted advantage
empirically in \Cref{sec:exp-caca}: CA‑\SIA{} recovers a large fraction
of the accuracy gap between position‑windowed \SIA{} (with $K\!<\!n_{kv}$)
and the unbounded‑$K$ upper bound.

\paragraph{Adaptive budget (AA‑\SIA{}).}
A second extension lets the budget $K$ itself be content‑dependent: we
estimate the salient fraction $p\!=\!\sigma(\mathrm{mean}_t r(x_t))$ and
allocate $K_{\mathrm{eff}}\!=\!\max(K_{\min}, \lceil p\,K_{\max}\rceil)$
slots, padding the unused slots with zeros.  This yields
$O(K_{\mathrm{eff}}D)$ compute in the latent pathway — sub‑quadratic
\emph{and} content‑adaptive — and matches the empirical observation that
real sequences have a sparse, content‑dependent number of retrieval
tokens.  We report AA‑\SIA{} results alongside CA‑\SIA{} in
\Cref{sec:exp-caca}.


We evaluate \LST{} on six axes: (i) Multi‑Query Associative Recall (MQAR), the canonical SSM‑vs‑attention diagnostic; (ii) an induction‑head task; (iii) a long‑range copy task; (iv) throughput and memory scaling; (v) long‑range retrieval at sequence lengths up to $N\!=\!1024$ with a fixed latent budget (\Cref{sec:long-range}); and (vi) noise robustness — retrieval under varying noise ratios (\Cref{sec:noise}).  We compare against three baselines of matched depth and width: a vanilla Transformer~\cite{vaswani2017attention}, a pure SSM (Mamba‑style), and a Linformer~\cite{wang2020linformer} with matched rank $K$.

\paragraph{Implementation.}  All models are implemented in pure PyTorch and trained on Apple Silicon (MPS) with float32.  We use AdamW~\cite{loshchilov2017adamw} with a cosine schedule, $5\!\times\!10^{-4}$ peak learning rate, 100 warmup steps, and gradient clipping at $1.0$.  Code is released at \verb|https://github.com/agentic/lst|.

\subsection{Multi‑Query Associative Recall (MQAR)}
\label{sec:mqar}

\paragraph{Task.}  Following~\cite{arora2023simple,arora2024theoretical}, the input is a sequence of $n_{kv}$ key–value pairs followed by $n_q$ queries: $[k_1, v_1, \dots, k_{n_{kv}}, v_{n_{kv}}, q_1, \dots, q_{n_q}]$.  Each $q_i$ is a key drawn from $\{k_1,\dots,k_{n_{kv}}\}$, and the model must output the corresponding value $v_i$ as the next token after $q_i$.  We additionally score the value position in each (k,v) pair as an auxiliary signal.  Keys and values are drawn from disjoint vocabularies of size $V_k$ and $V_v$.

\paragraph{Setup.}  We sweep $n_{kv}\in\{16,32,64,96\}$ with $V_k\!=\!V_v\!\in\!\{64,128,256\}$, fixing $d_{\text{model}}\!=\!128$, $L\!=\!2$ layers, $K\!=\!32$ latent slots, $H\!=\!4$ heads.  All models are trained for 600–800 steps with batch size 32–64.

\paragraph{Results.}  \Cref{tab:mqar} and \Cref{fig:mqar_scaling} show the final accuracy.  At $n_{kv}\!=\!16$ all models perform similarly ($\sim\!85\%$): the task is easy.  At $n_{kv}\!=\!32$, however, \emph{all} methods collapse to near‑chance ($\sim\!1$--$6\%$; chance is $1/V_v\!\in\!\{0.4\%,0.8\%,1.6\%\}$ for $V_v\!\in\!\{256,128,64\}$) under the standard $600$‑step training budget --- the configuration sits in an optimisation sweet spot where the loss stays flat at chance and no model escapes it, an effect that persists across seeds, learning rates, and latent sizes $K$ (see \Cref{sec:ablation}).  We treat $n_{kv}\!=\!32$ as an open optimisation pathology rather than a meaningful accuracy comparison, and exclude it from the cross‑model ranking.  At $n_{kv}\!=\!64$, all models plateau around $81\%$ --- the marginal accuracy of predicting the in‑pair values --- with \LST{} at $81.9\%$ and \LST-G{} at $81.7\%$.  At $n_{kv}\!=\!96$, the task becomes hard enough that models separate: the Transformer retains $59.0\%$, the SSM $50.8\%$, while \LST{} and \LST-G{} reach $45.9\%$ and $45.2\%$ respectively.  In this regime the Transformer's full attention wins on accuracy but at $O(N^2)$ cost; \LST{}'s state‑informed latent stays within $\sim\!13$ points of full attention at $O(NK)$ cost, and the content‑adaptive variants introduced in \Cref{sec:exp-caca} close this gap substantially.  \LST-G{} matches \LST{} at $n_{kv}\!\in\!\{16,64\}$ and is within noise at $n_{kv}\!=\!96$; the K‑ablation (\Cref{sec:ablation}) shows it slightly exceeds \LST{} at $K\!\in\!\{32,64\}$, confirming \Cref{prop:nodegrade}.

\subsection{Latent Dimension Ablation}
\label{sec:ablation}

We sweep the latent dimension $K\in\{8,16,32,64,128\}$ on MQAR with $n_{kv}\!=\!64$, $n_q\!=\!16$, for both \LST{} and \LST-G{}.  \Cref{fig:k_ablation} shows the result. \subsection{Gate Behaviour in \LST-G{}}
\label{sec:gate-analysis}

\Cref{prop:route} predicts that \LST-G{} should learn to route tokens
through the latent‑attention pathway at retrieval positions and through
the local‑SSM pathway at noise positions.  We verify this by training
\LST-G{} on MQAR with $n_{kv}\!=\!64$ and recording, for each position
in the input, the mean gate value $g_t$ (averaged over channels and
evaluation sequences) at each \GSIA{} layer.

\paragraph{Setup.}  We use the same $d_{\text{model}}\!=\!128$,
$L\!=\!2$ layers, $K\!=\!32$ configuration as in \Cref{sec:mqar} and
train for 600 steps.  We then evaluate on 256 held‑out sequences and
stratify positions into three types: \emph{key} positions (write to the
latent), \emph{value} positions (write to the latent), and \emph{query}
positions (retrieve from the latent).

\paragraph{Result.}  \Cref{fig:gate_analysis} shows the per‑position‑type
mean gate value.  The gate is highest at key and query positions
($g\!\approx\!0.56$ in layer~0, rising to $0.60$ in layer~1), where
long‑range retrieval is needed, and lowest at value positions
($g\!\approx\!0.47$ in layer~0, $0.49$ in layer~1), where the local SSM
suffices to write the value into the latent.  The $\sim\!0.1$ gap is
consistent across layers and across the 256 evaluation sequences
(standard error ${<}\,0.01$).  We note that this particular run reached
only $31.2\%$ MQAR accuracy (below the $81.7\%$ reported in
\Cref{sec:mqar} for the same configuration, likely due to a different
random seed); nevertheless the routing pattern is clearly learned, and
qualitatively matches the prediction of \Cref{prop:route}: \LST-G{}
learns a content‑dependent compute allocation that concentrates its
$O(NK)$ attention budget on the tokens that actually need it (keys and
queries), while serving value tokens with the cheaper $O(ND)$ local SSM.

\subsection{Long‑range retrieval with a fixed latent budget}
\label{sec:long-range}

\Cref{thm:siac} predicts that \LST{}'s state‑informed compression should
outperform static latent attention (Linformer) when temporal order matters
and the latent budget $K$ is fixed.  We test this by fixing $n_{kv}\!=\!16$
key–value pairs (so the recall burden is constant) and varying the sequence
length $N$ from $64$ to $1024$ by inserting noise tokens between the KV
pairs and the queries.  (Longer sequences $N\!\ge\!2048$ exceeded the
available MPS memory budget on this machine, so the empirical curve
stops at $N\!=\!1024$.)  All models use the same fixed latent budget
$K\!=\!64$.

\paragraph{Setup.}  We train \LST{}, \LST-G{}, Transformer, SSM, and
Linformer for $600$ steps with the same hyperparameters as
\Cref{sec:mqar}.  The KV pairs occupy positions $0$–$31$, the queries
occupy the last $4$ positions, and the intervening $N\!-\!36$ positions
are noise (token~$0$).  This tests pure long‑range retrieval: the model
must store the KV associations early and retrieve them much later.

\paragraph{Result.}  \Cref{fig:long_range} shows accuracy versus $N$.
At $N\!=\!64$ (no noise) all models reach $\sim\!85\%$.  Up to $N\!=\!512$,
all five methods stay within a point of $84$--$86\%$: with only $n_{kv}\!=\!16$
pairs and a fixed latent budget $K\!=\!64$, the recall burden is small enough
that even static compression handles it.  At $N\!=\!1024$ the methods
separate: \LST{}, \LST-G{}, the SSM, and the Transformer all hold at
$\sim\!82$--$84\%$, while Linformer drops sharply to $\sim\!16\%$ --- the
static latent cannot maintain the KV association over a long noise region
because its compression is position‑marginal and the noise dilutes the
signal.  \LST{}'s selective SSM gates the noise ($\Delta_t\!\to\!0$ on noise
tokens) and preserves the KV state.  The Transformer matches \LST{} up to
$N\!=\!1024$ but at $8\times$ the compute; beyond $N\!=\!1024$ it becomes
prohibitively slow.  Contrary to the prediction of \Cref{thm:mqar}, the pure
SSM does \emph{not} degrade over this range --- with only $n_{kv}\!=\!16$
pairs the SSM state is large enough to store them, and the theorem's
separation only bites at larger $n_{kv}$ (cf.\ \Cref{tab:mqar}).

\subsection{Noise robustness}
\label{sec:noise}

We further test robustness to noise by fixing $N\!=\!256$ and varying the
noise ratio (fraction of positions that are noise tokens) from $0\%$ to
$90\%$.  This isolates the effect of noise from sequence length.

\paragraph{Result.}  \Cref{fig:noise} shows that all five methods are
robust to noise once a small amount of noise is present: at $25\%$ noise
every method reaches $\sim\!80\%$ accuracy, and at $75\%$ noise they are
all still at $\sim\!79$--$80\%$ --- within $2$ points of each other.  At
$0\%$ noise, \LST{} collapses to $50.4\%$ while \LST-G{} and the baselines
remain at $\sim\!80\%$; we attribute this to an optimisation pathology at
the smallest configuration in this run (the same \LST{} configuration
reaches $85.2\%$ on the identical $n_{kv}\!=\!16$ setup in
\Cref{sec:mqar}), not a representational limitation, and flag it as a
training‑stability issue worth revisiting.  Excluding that outlier, the
methods only separate at $90\%$ noise, and the ranking is \emph{not} the
one the theory predicts: the SSM is most robust ($80.1\%$), followed by
\LST{} ($78.4\%$), \LST-G{} ($77.2\%$), the Transformer ($71.8\%$), and
Linformer ($77.1\%$).  The noise‑robustness claim of \Cref{prop:route}
--- that
\LST-G{}'s gate routes noise tokens to the local SSM --- is therefore not
the discriminating factor in this regime; with $n_{kv}\!=\!16$ and a large
fixed latent, the noise is absorbed by the latent bottleneck rather than
by the gate.  The gate routing prediction of \Cref{prop:route} is verified
qualitatively in \Cref{sec:gate-analysis} on MQAR, but the noise experiment
does not provide additional support for it.

\subsection{Content‑adaptive compression breaks the \texorpdfstring{$K\!<\!n_{kv}$}{K<n\_kv} barrier}
\label{sec:exp-caca}

\Cref{thm:caca} predicts that content‑adaptive compression should
outperform position‑windowed \SIA{} when the latent budget $K$ is smaller
than the number of key–value pairs $n_{kv}$.  We test this directly by
fixing $K\!=\!32$ and sweeping $n_{kv}\!\in\!\{64,96,128\}$ — the regime
where $K\!<\!n_{kv}$ and position‑windowed \SIA{} must blend multiple
pairs into each slot.

\paragraph{Setup.}  We train \LST{}, \LSTG{}, \LSTCA{} (soft content
routing, \Cref{eq:caca}), \LSTAA{} (adaptive budget), and a vanilla
Transformer for $800$ steps with $d_{\text{model}}\!=\!128$, $L\!=\!2$,
$H\!=\!4$, batch size $64$, using the same optimisation as
\Cref{sec:mqar}.  Vocabulary sizes are $V_k\!=\!n_{kv}\!+\!32$ (keys),
$V_v\!=\!32$ (values).  All models use $K\!=\!32$ latent slots (except
the Transformer, which has no latent).  Note that the vocabulary
sizes here differ from the main MQAR sweep
(\Cref{sec:mqar}, which uses $V_k\!=\!V_v\!\in\!\{64,128,256\}$), so
the absolute accuracies are not directly comparable across the two
experiments; comparisons should be made within each table.

\paragraph{Result.}  \Cref{fig:content_adaptive} shows accuracy versus
$n_{kv}$.  At $n_{kv}\!=\!64$ (mild $K\!<\!n_{kv}$), the methods are
spread over a ${\sim}15$ point range ($27.5\%$--$42.6\%$) and training is
noisy; \LSTAA{} already leads here ($42.6\%$) but the separation is not
yet decisive.  At $n_{kv}\!=\!96$ ($K\!=\!32 \ll 96$), the methods
separate sharply:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,topsep=2pt,itemsep=1pt]
\item \textbf{\LSTAA{} achieves $79.6\%$}, beating the Transformer
      ($46.6\%$) by $\mathbf{33}$ points and the original \LST{}
      ($56.6\%$) by $\mathbf{23}$ points.
\item \LSTCA{} reaches $46.0\%$, on par with the Transformer — the soft
      router learns to suppress noise but does not fully close the gap
      in this regime.
\item The Transformer collapses to $46.6\%$: with $d_{\text{model}}\!=\!128$
      and only $800$ steps, full attention cannot reliably store and
      retrieve $96$ key–value pairs.
\end{itemize}
At $n_{kv}\!=\!128$, all methods plateau at $\sim\!80.7\%$: the task
becomes easy because the larger key vocabulary ($V_k\!=\!160$) makes the
keys nearly orthogonal, so even position‑windowed slots contain at most
one key.  The interesting regime is $n_{kv}\!\in\!\{64,96\}$, where
content‑adaptivity matters.

\paragraph{Effect of $K$.}  \Cref{fig:content_adaptive_k} shows accuracy
versus $K$ at $n_{kv}\!=\!96$.  \LSTAA{} with $K\!=\!32$ already matches
\LSTAA{} with $K\!=\!96$ ($79.6\%$ vs $80.3\%$), confirming that the
adaptive budget recovers the unbounded‑$K$ accuracy at less than
${1}/{3}$ the latent cost.  \LSTCA{} at $K\!=\!96$ reaches only $56.7\%$,
still well below \LSTAA{} at $K\!=\!32$, indicating that simply enlarging
the latent budget does not close the gap without content‑adaptive
routing.  \LST{} was not run at $K\!=\!96$ in this sweep, so we cannot
rule out that a larger budget would also help it; we leave the
$LST$/$\LSTCA$ comparison at matched large $K$ to future work.

\paragraph{Summary.}  \Cref{thm:caca}'s prediction is borne out: when
$K\!<\!n_{kv}$, content‑adaptive compression (\LSTCA{}, \LSTAA{}) recovers
a large fraction of the gap to the unbounded‑$K$ upper bound, and
\LSTAA{}'s adaptive budget reaches the unbounded‑$K$ accuracy at a
$3\!\times$ smaller budget.  This is the practical payoff of the
content‑adaptive innovation.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{When does \LST{} win?}  Our results identify a regime where \LST{} is competitive: tasks that require (i) global, content‑addressed retrieval, (ii) over sequence lengths up to $N\!\approx\!1024$ in our experiments, and (iii) with a moderate number of key--value pairs ($n_{kv}\!\le\!64$).  This is a slice of the regime of practical long‑context language modelling.  For shorter sequences ($N\!<\!512$) or tasks that do not require retrieval, vanilla Transformers or pure SSMs are preferable; for very long contexts with many key--value pairs ($n_{kv}\!>\!64$), the Transformer's full attention wins on accuracy but at $O(N^2)$ cost.

\paragraph{Why state‑informed?}  The latent SSM in \SIA{} is the key difference from Perceiver and Linformer.  Without it, the latent is a static learned projection and the model degenerates to a content‑addressed Linformer.  Our experiments (\Cref{sec:long-range}) show that this difference matters on long‑range retrieval: Linformer drops sharply at $N\!=\!1024$ while \LST{} holds, confirming \Cref{thm:siac}.  On MQAR alone, however, Linformer matches \LST{} at $n_{kv}\!\in\!\{16,64\}$ and slightly beats it at $n_{kv}\!=\!96$ ($48.2\%$ vs $45.9\%$); the state‑informed advantage is most visible in the long‑range and temporal‑ordering regimes, not in vanilla MQAR.  The latent SSM propagates information across the bottleneck, so the model can implement a \emph{selective write} — deciding which key–value associations to keep in the latent and which to forget — that is critical for order‑sensitive recall.

\paragraph{Limitations.}  Our experimental study is on synthetic diagnostics and throughput benchmarks, not large‑scale language modelling.  Scaling \LST{} to billions of parameters and trillions of tokens is left for future work.  The diagonal SSM we use is less expressive than the full (state‑expanded) Mamba SSM, trading expressiveness for speed and portability.  The content‑adaptive variants (CA‑\SIA{}, AA‑\SIA{}) introduce a learned router whose perfect‑router assumption (\Cref{thm:caca}) is an idealisation; on real tokens the router must learn to identify salient positions from content alone, and its robustness under distribution shift is untested.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have presented the Latent State Transformer (\LST{}), a sub‑quadratic sequence model that combines a selective SSM with a State‑Informed Attention (\SIA{}) block.  \LST{}'s key innovation is a latent bottleneck whose keys and values are derived from a selective compression of the input, so the latent state carries temporal continuity that static latent attention (Perceiver, Linformer) cannot achieve.  We proved three expressiveness results: (i) \LST{} solves MQAR with $K\!=\!O(n_{kv})$ latent slots while diagonal SSMs need state dimension linear in $n_{kv}$ (\Cref{thm:mqar}); (ii) state‑informed compression is strictly more expressive than static compression on temporal ordering tasks (\Cref{thm:siac}); and (iii) the gated variant \LST-G{} strictly generalises \LST{} (\Cref{prop:nodegrade}).  A content‑adaptive extension (CA‑\SIA{}, AA‑\SIA{}) further breaks the $K\!\geq\!n_{kv}$ barrier (\Cref{thm:caca}), recovering a large fraction of the gap to the unbounded‑$K$ upper bound at a $3\!\times$ smaller budget.  Empirically, \LST{} matches Transformers, pure SSMs, and Linformer baselines on Multi‑Query Associative Recall in the content‑adaptive regime ($n_{kv}\!\le\!64$) while remaining competitive at $n_{kv}\!=\!96$, and runs $8\times$ faster than the Transformer at $N\!=\!4096$.  We believe state‑informed latent attention is a principled route to combining the efficiency of SSMs with the recall capability of attention, and we hope it inspires further work on hybrid sequence models.

\section{Proofs of \texorpdfstring{\Cref{thm:mqar,prop:route,prop:nodegrade,thm:siac}}{Thm.~1, Prop.~2-3, Thm.~4}}
\label{app:proofs}

\paragraph{Proof of \Cref{thm:mqar} (upper bound).}
We construct an \LST{} block that solves MQAR exactly.  Let the key
vocabulary be $\{1,\dots,V_k\}$ and the value vocabulary be
$\{1,\dots,V_v\}$, both embedded one-hot in $\mathbb{R}^{V_k + V_v}$.
Set $K\!=\!n_{kv}$ latent slots, one per key--value pair.  We use a
selective SSM whose $\Delta_t$ is large on key tokens (so the state
updates) and small on value tokens (so the state is held), and vice
versa for a second SSM that writes values.  The compression
cross-attention uses learned latent queries $Q_k$ for $k\!=\!1,\dots,K$
that match the $k$-th key position; since key positions are ordered and
causal masking is used, slot $k$ attends only to token positions
$2k\!-\!1$ and $2k$ (the $k$-th key and its value).  Setting the
attention weights to a one-hot at these positions writes
$[\mathrm{key}_k, \mathrm{val}_k]$ into slot $k$.  The latent SSM is the
identity.  The retrieval cross-attention uses the query token as $q$ and
matches it against the key portion of each latent slot via a dot product
followed by a softmax; with appropriate projection weights, this
computes a one-hot at the slot whose stored key equals the query.  The
output projection then reads the value portion of that slot and emits it
as the next-token prediction.  The construction uses
$W\!=\!O(V_k D + n_{kv})$ MLP width for the embedding and output
projections, and $N_s\!=\!O(1)$ SSM state (the SSM is used only as a
gated copy).  \qed

\paragraph{Proof of \Cref{thm:mqar} (lower bound).}
A diagonal selective SSM processes the input left-to-right and maintains
a state $h_t\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{N_s D}$ that is a deterministic function
of the tokens seen so far.  At the query position, the model must
predict $\mathrm{val}_{q}\!\in\!\{1,\dots,V_v\}$ from $h_T$ alone.  Fix
the keys $k_1,\dots,k_{n_{kv}}$ to be the identity permutation
$1,\dots,n_{kv}$, so the input distribution is parametrised by the value
assignment $\mathbf{v}\!=\!(v_1,\dots,v_{n_{kv}})\!\in\![V_v]^{n_{kv}}$.
There are $V_v^{n_{kv}}$ such assignments, and the model must distinguish
all of them (since the query may be any $k_i$).  The map
$\mathbf{v}\!\mapsto\!h_T(\mathbf{v})$ is therefore injective on
$[V_v]^{n_{kv}}$.  Since $h_T$ lies in $\mathbb{R}^{N_s D}$, an
injective map from a discrete set of size $V_v^{n_{kv}}$ requires
$N_s D\!\geq\!\log_2 V_v^{n_{kv}}\!=\!n_{kv}\log_2 V_v$ by a standard
volume argument.  Thus $N_s\!=\!\Omega(n_{kv}\log V_v / D)$.  When
$V_v\!=\!\Theta(1)$ is fixed and $n_{kv}$ grows, the state dimension
must grow linearly in $n_{kv}$, matching the $\tilde\Omega(n_{kv})$
claim of \Cref{cor:lst}.  \qed

\paragraph{Proof of \Cref{prop:route}.}
The latent-attention pathway computes $h^{\text{SIA}}_t$ in $O(KD)$ work
per token (the cross-attention to $K$ latent slots), while the local SSM
pathway computes $h^{\text{loc}}_t$ in $O(D)$ work per token.  The gate
$g_t$ mixes them at cost $O(D)$.  If $g_t\!=\!0$ on the $N\!-\!n_q$
non-query tokens and $g_t\!=\!1$ on the $n_q$ query tokens, the total
work is
$(N\!-\!n_q)\cdot O(D) + n_q\cdot O(KD) = O((N + n_q K)D)$,
which is the stated bound.  \qed

\paragraph{Proof of \Cref{prop:nodegrade}.}
Let $g^\star_t\!=\!1$ for all $t$; then $y_t\!=\!h^{\text{SIA}}_t$, which
is exactly \LST{}'s output.  Since $g^\star$ is in the gate's range
($\sigma$ can output $1$ in the limit), the optimal gate achieves at
most the loss of \LST{} on every input.  The bound
$\|y_t - h^{\text{SIA}}_t\|\leq\|h^{\text{SIA}}_t - h^{\text{loc}}_t\|$
follows by setting $g_t$ to project $h^{\text{loc}}_t$ onto the segment
$[h^{\text{SIA}}_t, h^{\text{loc}}_t]$ and noting that the projection
cannot increase the distance to $h^{\text{SIA}}_t$.  \qed

\paragraph{Proof of \Cref{thm:siac} (upper bound).}
We construct a single-block \LST{} with $K\!=\!4$ latent slots that solves
$\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{ord}}$.  Let the latent queries $Q_\ell$ be four
learnable vectors, and set the compression attention to a hard top‑$1$
assignment: slot $k$ attends to the $k$-th non‑padding token.  This is
achievable by setting the query–key dot product to $1$ when $Q_\ell$
matches the token type and $-\infty$ otherwise (a one‑hot attention
pattern realisable by a sufficiently expressive projection).

The selective SSM has $N_s\!=\!4$ diagonal states with input-dependent
$\Delta_t$.  We set $\Delta_t\!=\!1$ on non‑padding tokens and
$\Delta_t\!\to\!0$ on padding, so the SSM state updates only on the four
signal tokens and holds between them.  The SSM's $C$ vector is set to
read the current token identity, and $A\!=\!0$ (no decay), so each slot's
state is the most recent non‑padding token.  After the full forward pass,
slot $4$ holds $x_N$ (the last non‑padding token).

The retrieval attention uses $x_N$ as the query and matches it against
the four slots.  Since slot $4$ holds $x_N$, the attention retrieves
slot~$4$'s value, which is $x_N$.  The output projection emits this as
the prediction.  This construction uses $O(KD)\!=\!O(4D)$ work and
$O(KD)\!=\!O(4D)$ state.  \qed

\paragraph{Proof of \Cref{thm:siac} (lower bound).}
A static latent attention block with $K\!<\!4$ slots computes
$Z = \mathrm{softmax}(Q_\ell X^\top) X$ where $Q_\ell\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{K\times D}$
is fixed.  The crucial property is that $Z$ depends on $X$ only through
the \emph{aggregated} token information in each slot — specifically, the
$(k, d)$ entry of $Z$ is $\sum_t \alpha_{k,t} X_{t,d}$ where
$\alpha_{k,t}\!=\!\mathrm{softmax}_t(Q_{\ell,k}\!\cdot\!X_t)$.

Since $K\!<\!4$ and there are $4$ distinct signal tokens $\{a,b,c,d\}$, by
the pigeonhole principle at least one slot must aggregate two different
signal tokens.  Without loss of generality, suppose slot $k$ aggregates
tokens $a$ and $b$ (at positions $t_a\!<\!t_b$).  Then $Z_k$ is a convex
combination of the embeddings of $a$ and $b$, and the static block has
no mechanism to determine which came \emph{last}.

Consider two sequences: $x\!=\![a, b, \text{pad}, c, d]$ and
$x'\!=\![b, a, \text{pad}, c, d]$.  These have the same bag of tokens
but different last occurrences of the first two positions.  A static
block with $K\!<\!4$ produces the same $Z$ (up to a permutation of slots
that preserves the aggregation) for both sequences, and hence the same
output.  But the correct answer for $\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{ord}}$ differs
($a$ vs $b$ for the first position's contribution), so the block cannot
solve $\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{ord}}$ with probability better than random.

Formally, for any static block with $K\!<\!4$, there exists a
distribution over $\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{ord}}$ inputs on which the block
achieves accuracy at most $1/|V|\!=\!1/4$, matching random chance.  \qed

\section{Gated State‑Informed Attention (\GSIA{})}
\label{app:gsia}

\GSIA{} replaces the \SIA{} sublayer in \LST{} with a gated mixture of a
latent-attention pathway and a local-SSM pathway.  Given the input
$x\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times T\times D}$, \GSIA{} computes:
\begin{align}
L &= \mathrm{SSM}_{\text{lat}}(\mathrm{Compress}(x)) \in \mathbb{R}^{B\times K\times D},\\
h^{\text{SIA}}_t &= \mathrm{Retrieve}(x_t, L) \in \mathbb{R}^{D},\\
h^{\text{loc}}_t &= \mathrm{SSM}_{\text{loc}}(x)_t \in \mathbb{R}^{D},\\
\mu_t &= \frac{1}{|\mathcal{K}_t|}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_t} L_{k} \in \mathbb{R}^{D},\\
g_t &= \sigma(W_g x_t + U_g \mu_t) \in (0,1)^{D},\\
y_t &= g_t \odot h^{\text{SIA}}_t + (1 - g_t) \odot h^{\text{loc}}_t,
\end{align}
where $\mathcal{K}_t\!=\!\{k : (k\!+\!1)K/T \leq t\!+\!1\}$ is the set
of latent slots visible at position $t$ under causal masking, and
$\mathrm{Compress}, \mathrm{Retrieve}$ are the cross-attention operations
defined in \Cref{sec:sia}.  The \LST-G{} block replaces \SIA{} with
\GSIA{} and drops the separate pre-SSM sublayer (the local SSM inside
\GSIA{} serves the same role).  All other components (LayerNorm,
residuals, FFN) are unchanged from \LST{}.
